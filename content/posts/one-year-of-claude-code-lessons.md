<p>A year ago, Claude Code — an AI tool that does programming work from a simple text instruction — was an internal experiment whose first demo in the company Slack got, by the team's own admission, two reactions. Today it's used by developers and organisations worldwide.</p>

<p>In <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hth_tLaC2j8" rel="noopener">an eighteen-minute retrospective on Anthropic's Claude channel</a>, Boris Cherny and Cat Wu — who lead the product — look back at that year. It's aimed at engineers, but four of their themes translate straight into plain business language.</p>

<h2>1. Verification is what makes AI trustworthy</h2>
<p>A chunk of the conversation covers "how to make Claude good at verification" — teaching the AI to check its own work against reality rather than just sounding confident. The business translation: <strong>never judge AI output by how convincing it reads</strong>; judge it by whether it can be checked. Whatever you automate, build in the checking step — a human review, a test, a comparison against the source.</p>

<h2>2. Routines beat one-off requests</h2>
<p>They talk about "routines and loops" — recurring jobs the AI runs on schedule, like reviewing code or monitoring systems. The pattern is bigger than programming: the highest-value automation isn't the question you ask once, it's the job that repeats forever. Weekly reports, follow-up sequences, daily checks. Set the routine once; collect the hours back every week. (This is <a href="/blog/delegate-tasks-to-ai-claude-cowork">delegation rather than chatting</a>, and it's where the compounding lives.)</p>

<h2>3. Roles are merging</h2>
<p>One of their observations: Claude Code is being used well beyond engineers, and job boundaries are blurring — product people ship things, and the interesting question becomes "is the future product or engineering?". In a small business you've always been the marketer-accountant-receptionist anyway; AI just makes each hat cheaper to wear. The owner who "can't do design" can now get a decent flyer drafted; the one who "isn't technical" can get a working prototype. The excuses list shrinks every quarter.</p>

<h2>4. Less context beats more — "context minimalism"</h2>
<p>The team describes moving from stuffing the AI with everything ("context engineering") to giving it only what the task needs. Anyone who's briefed a human freelancer knows this instinctively: a tight brief gets better work than a rambling one. When you use AI, resist pasting your life story. State the job, the audience, the constraint, the example — stop.</p>
