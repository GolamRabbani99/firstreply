A catering owner I work with once showed me her lead ads dashboard with genuine confusion. Forty-two leads that month. Cost per lead: about £11. Enquiries that turned into actual conversations: six.

Her first instinct was the one everyone has — the leads must be rubbish. Facebook's fault. Wrong audience, tyre-kickers, people filling in forms by accident.

Then we looked at one thing: the time between each lead arriving and her first reply. The average was just over four hours. For enquiries that landed after 6pm, it was the next morning. The leads weren't rubbish. They were cold by the time anyone spoke to them.

## Leads don't age like wine

Here's the uncomfortable truth about someone who fills in your form from a Facebook ad: at that exact moment, you have their full attention. They're thinking about their event, their child's tutoring, their wedding menu. They might be sitting on the sofa comparing three companies at once.

An hour later, they're doing something else entirely. The moment has passed. Research on lead response has shown the same pattern for well over a decade: the odds of actually reaching an enquiry collapse within the first hour, and keep collapsing from there. Not because people are flaky — because attention moves on and, more importantly, **because someone else answered first**.

> The first business to have a real conversation usually wins the job. Not the best one. The first one.

That's the 60-minute rule. If your average first response takes longer than an hour, you're not really competing for that lead — you're hoping nobody else replied yet.

## Where the four hours actually go

Nobody decides to ignore leads for four hours. It happens structurally:

- **The lead lands somewhere nobody looks.** Facebook lead ads deliver into a downloadable CSV or a "Leads Centre" that most owners check weekly, if ever. Website forms go to an inbox that also receives invoices, spam and newsletters.
- **The enquiry arrives at the worst possible time.** If you run a catering company, enquiries peak exactly when you're mid-service. Education businesses get them in the evening, after parents finish work. The busier your business, the slower your replies — which punishes you for being good.
- **Replying takes real effort.** A proper reply means reading the enquiry, remembering your availability, writing something that doesn't sound canned. That's a ten-minute job, so it gets deferred. Deferred twice, it becomes tomorrow.

None of this is a discipline problem. It's a systems problem wearing a discipline costume.

## Three fixes you can make this week (no software required)

### 1. Make leads impossible to miss

Turn on instant notifications for every lead source and send them somewhere you actually look — for most owners that's WhatsApp or SMS, not email. If your form only emails you, add your phone number's email-to-SMS gateway or a free Zapier/n8n hook. The goal: you *know* within a minute, every time.

### 2. Pre-write your first reply

Ninety per cent of a good first response is the same every time: thank them, confirm you can help, ask the one question that moves things forward, sign off like a human. Write it once, properly, in your own voice. When a lead arrives you're editing, not composing — a two-minute job instead of ten.

### 3. Decide your follow-up cadence in advance

No reply doesn't mean no. People miss messages. A polite nudge on day one, day three and day seven roughly doubles the number of enquiries that turn into conversations — but only if it actually happens, which means deciding the schedule before you need it, not improvising per lead.
